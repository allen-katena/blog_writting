Humanoid robotics grew up this fall, and were in the room at Singapore's first humanoid fight night. Three things changed:

𝟭. 𝗠𝗼𝗻𝗲𝘆 𝗻𝗼𝘄 𝗳𝗼𝗹𝗹𝗼𝘄𝘀 𝗿𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗻𝘂𝗲
China reportedly told banks to raise the bar for humanoid IPOs after Unitree fell ~55% from its peak. Meanwhile, Agility heads to Nasdaq with $300M+ in committed orders, Hyundai plans 25,000 Atlas units, and IDC expects 750,000 humanoids a year by 2030.
The question is no longer whether humanoids will ship. It is who can make them pay.

𝟮. 𝗛𝘂𝗺𝗮𝗻 𝘀𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝗶𝘀 𝗯𝗲𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝘁
Boston Dynamics' new Atlas hand ran its drill demo with a remote operator. Mecka, which sells human movement data to robot labs, raised $60M led by Sequoia and reports a $100M+ revenue run-rate. Minerva raised ~$10M to build a humanoid that specialists pilot in VR for bomb disposal and HazMat work.
Language models learned from text people had already written. Robots have to learn from human movement that was never recorded, and that data is now a paid market.

𝟯. 𝗘𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘁𝗮𝗶𝗻𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗼𝘀𝘁 𝗽𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗽𝗮𝘁𝗵 𝘁𝗼 𝗿𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗻𝘂𝗲 𝘁𝗼𝗱𝗮𝘆
AGIBOT and Chimelong opened a theme park with 300+ humanoids. EngineAI, maker of the T800, runs its own fight league, URKL, with a gold belt worth ~$1.4M. ARC Night stood out: it was Singapore's first full-sized humanoid fight night, and every bout was teleoperated in real time. Muay Thai, MMA and boxing champions in motion-capture suits piloted humanoids move for move for 380+ people. Only machines took the hits.

Live entertainment ties it all together: audiences pay now while factory fleets ramp, it is the toughest public test of the hardware, and it produces the hardest human data to collect: fast, contact-heavy decisions under pressure.

Building humanoid bodies is now a crowded, well-funded race. Teaching them human skill is still early, and that is where the opportunity is.

ARC's next fight night will be bigger, louder and harder-hitting. Stay tuned.
